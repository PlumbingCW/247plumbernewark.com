Most faucet calls are not emergencies, and a slow drip can wait for a daytime visit. The urgent case is a valve that cannot be closed, because that leaves you choosing between running water and no water at all.

Shutoff valve that will not close: stop the flow first

Every sink, toilet and appliance should have its own small shutoff, called an angle stop or supply stop. Old ones seize from mineral buildup and corrosion, and the stem packing wears out. When you finally turn one that has sat untouched for years, it can start to weep or fail to seal at all.

If a fixture valve will not close, do not force it with pliers. A stem that snaps off leaves an open pipe. Close the main water valve instead, then call for a plumber who can replace the stop with a new quarter-turn valve, which is far less likely to seize.

The main valve can fail too. Gate valves in older plumbing sometimes jam half open, and the City of Newark will shut the water off at the curb when a repair is scheduled. If the main is stuck, the plumber can swap it for a full-port ball valve while the water is off.

  1. Close the main water valveTurn it clockwise slowly until it stops, and never hammer it shut.
  2. Open the lowest faucetThis drains the line so the leak slows.
  3. Catch the dripPut a bucket or towels under the fixture and keep power cords away from the water.
  4. Photograph the damageTake pictures of wet walls, floors and the fixture for any claim.
  5. Do not force itA broken stem turns a drip into a flood.

When to call a plumber right away

  • The fixture valve and the main both refuse to close, so water cannot be stopped at all.
  • A stem has snapped off or a supply line has let go and water is spraying. Close the main if you can reach it.
  • Water is running toward an outlet, a light fixture or a ceiling fan. Stay clear and switch breakers off only if the floor and panel are dry, otherwise call the electric utility.
  • Shower temperature suddenly swings to scalding hot, which means the mixing valve inside the wall is failing.

Which faucet do you have?

The repair depends on how the faucet controls water. Knowing the type tells the plumber which part to bring.

Faucet typeHow it worksCommon failure
CompressionRubber washer presses on a seat with a screw handleWorn washer or pitted seat, steady drip
CartridgeOne cartridge slides to mix and shut offWorn o-rings, handle leaks or stiffness
BallSlotted ball with rubber seals under one handleWorn seals and springs, leak at the base
Ceramic discTwo polished discs rotate over each otherMineral grit in discs, slow weeping

Compression faucets are the easiest to rebuild, while ceramic disc units often just need a new cartridge. Matching the exact model matters because parts are not interchangeable between brands. If the fixture is old enough that parts are hard to find, replacement is usually cheaper than another repair.

Shower and tub problems

A shower valve that drips after you turn it off, or a showerhead that leaks at the arm, may be a worn cartridge or a loose connection. Behind the wall is where it gets serious. If a tub spout leaks only when the shower runs, the diverter inside it is worn, and a new spout is a quick fix.

A mixing valve that gives sudden hot or cold swings has a worn balancing spool or cartridge. Slow staining on a ceiling below the bathroom suggests the problem is inside the wall, and opening the wall for access is a separate decision from the valve repair.

Slow tub or shower drains are a different problem from a leaking faucet. Hair and soap build up in the trap, and a plumber can clear it. If that trouble keeps returning or spreads to other fixtures, the next page to read is drain cleaning.

Repair or replace the fixture

Repairing a good fixture with a new cartridge or washers usually takes one short visit. Replace the whole faucet when the finish is flaking, the body is corroded, parts are no longer made or the same leak keeps returning. A cheap faucet can cost more in repeat visits than a better one costs once.

Replacement also gives you a chance to install new supply lines and a fresh shutoff. If the supply hoses are older than the faucet, change them together, because older braided lines can fail without warning. A dripping toilet connection found at the same time can be handled with toilet repair.

Pipes behind the fixture

Sometimes the faucet is fine and the problem is the line feeding it. A supply pipe that has cracked or split, or a joint that gave way, sprays water inside a cabinet or wall. That is no longer a fixture job. If water is moving fast and the main valve is closed, you may be looking at burst pipe repair, where the fix is cutting out the damaged section.

Who may do the work

A homeowner exemption exists in state law for plumbing that is not gas piping, in an owner's own non-rental home, with a permit application on file. Anything else needs a licensed plumber.

Replacing a faucet in kind is a fixture swap, but moving a valve or pipe is not, so permit rules can change with the scope. Edition questions come up here, since the state follows the 2021 IPC and the City's code adopts the 2018 edition, so ask the permit office which applies if the job moves a valve. Verify any license at delpros.delaware.gov before work starts.

Battery Backup Sump Pumps for Newark, DE Basements

When the power drops in a storm, a battery backup keeps the water out of a Newark, DE basement. Here is how to choose and maintain one.

Written by Newark 24/7 Plumbing · 2026

Open 24/7

Call now

A battery backup sump pump is a second pump, wired to its own battery and charger, that takes over when the main pump loses power or fails. In a Newark, DE basement it matters most during the exact storms that cause flooding, because heavy rain and power outages tend to arrive together.

A main sump pump runs on household current. When the lights go out, it stops, and the pit fills at the moment it is filling fastest. The backup is the only thing left working.

How a battery backup actually works

The standard setup has three pieces: a second pump in the same pit or a nearby one, a deep-cycle battery in a vented box and a charger-controller that keeps the battery topped up. The controller watches the water level with its own float switch. If the water climbs higher than the main pump's switch point, the backup starts.

That last detail is a feature. A backup sits idle until the main pump fails to hold the water, so it also covers a stuck float, a burned-out motor and a tripped breaker. A backup is not only for blackouts.

The backup pump is usually smaller than the main one. It buys time, not full capacity, which is why runtime matters as much as power.

Backup types compared

Water-powered units draw on the home's water pressure, so ask the plumber how much water the unit uses and where its discharge goes. The discharge matters here as much as it does for the main pump.

How long a battery lasts in a storm

Runtime depends on how hard the pump works, and that depends on how fast water enters the pit. A battery that runs a pump for many hours at a slow trickle may last far shorter at full inflow. The maker's chart shows hours at a given flow, so ask for it and compare it to what your pit sees in a wet week.

The battery itself ages. Most deep-cycle batteries lose capacity after a few years, and heat in a utility room shortens that further. Follow the manufacturer's replacement schedule, and test the system on a calendar, not when the sky turns dark.

Add an alarm. A high-water alarm or a battery-low alarm gives you notice before the basement does.

What Newark, DE storms ask of a backup

Federal climate normals for 1991 to 2020 at the Newark Ag Farm station (NOAA) put average yearly precipitation near 47 inches. About 13 days a year bring an inch of rain or more, and about two bring two inches or more.

Those heavy days are the ones that fill pits. Inside City limits, Newark runs its own electric utility, so outages are handled by City of Newark Electric Department crews. Outside the City, ask your own electric provider.

For you, the lesson is to size runtime for a long, wet day, not a short shower. A backup that survives a two-inch storm only if the power stays on is not a backup.

The City's own stormwater page blames much local flooding on storm pipes and inlets that cannot keep up with intense rain. In a storm like that, many streets and yards are wet at once. A backup that was fine in a dry summer can run for hours.

Where the backup discharges

The backup needs a path out of the house too. Newark's code keeps sump water out of the sanitary sewer, so the line must end outdoors or in the storm system where the code permits. The sewer chapter names plumbers as well as owners, so a plumber who suggests tying into a drain is suggesting something prohibited.

Ask the plumber to show where the backup line ends. If it joins the main discharge, a check valve must stop the main pump from pushing water back through the idle backup.

Two pumps feeding one small pipe can also choke the flow when you need it most.

Installation and testing steps

  1. Check the pitA shared pit needs room for two pumps and two floats without tangling. Some pits are too small.
  2. Set the switch heightsThe backup float sits above the main pump's float, so the backup starts only after the main pump has failed to keep up.
  3. Place the battery and chargerKeep them on a shelf or stand above any water, in a spot with air movement and away from heat.
  4. Wire the alarmConnect a high-water and low-battery alarm you will actually hear.
  5. Run a full testFill the pit with a hose until each float trips, and confirm each pump moves the water outside.
  6. Test on a scheduleRepeat the test every few months and before storm season, and replace the battery on the maker's timetable.

A plumber installing the pump handles the plumbing part. The electrical connection must be safe and grounded, so ask who is responsible for it before the work starts. For wider scope, see plumbing installation.

If your basement already floods

A backup keeps a pump running, but it cannot fix a pit that is too small or a lot that sends water toward the house. If water still arrives faster than any pump can move it, the cure is drainage work around the foundation. The exterior options appear under basement waterproofing.

Whatever you do, keep people out of a basement with water near outlets or the panel. Turn off power at the main breaker only if you can reach it without stepping in water. Otherwise call for help, because a plumber or an electrician has the right gear.

Insurance: a backup is not the same as cover

A pump that keeps running prevents a loss, but it does not pay for one. Ask your agent three plain questions: is flood excluded, is sewer or drain backup covered by a rider, and what does a separate flood policy require before it starts. The City of Newark points owners to the National Flood Insurance Program, and says most homeowners' policies leave flood out.

Cover bought after the water arrives will not help, because flood policies carry a waiting period. Settle the paperwork in a dry month.

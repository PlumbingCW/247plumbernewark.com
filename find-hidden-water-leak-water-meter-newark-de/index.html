How to Find a Hidden Water Leak With Your Water Meter in Newark, DE

A quiet house, a meter that keeps moving, and a 15-minute test that tells you whether water is leaking before you open a wall.

Written by Newark 24/7 Plumbing · 2026

Open 24/7

If you suspect a hidden leak in your Newark, DE home, your water meter can confirm it in about 15 minutes. Turn off every tap and appliance that uses water, then watch the meter. A dial that creeps forward, or a small leak indicator that keeps flickering, means water is escaping somewhere past the meter.

You do not need tools for this test, only a flashlight and a clear head. It will not tell you where the leak is, but it tells you whether you have one, and whether it is likely inside the house or in the pipe running to it. That alone saves a lot of guessing.

If the meter proves a leak and you cannot find the source, a plumber doing leak detection in Newark, DE can trace it with listening gear and pressure tests before anyone cuts into drywall or digs up a yard.

Run the meter test

Plan for a time when nobody is showering, running the dishwasher or washing clothes. Ice makers and automatic humidifiers also draw water, so switch those off or note them. The test works best when the house is quiet for the full quarter hour.

  1. Find the meter and clear the lidLook in the basement, utility room or a ground-level box, depending on how your home is plumbed. If you cannot find it, ask the City before you start digging around.
  2. Close every water useClose each faucet, pause the laundry and dishes, and ask everyone to hold off on flushing. Do not touch the main valve yet.
  3. Read the meter and write it downNote every digit on the register. Look for the small triangle, star or flow indicator, which spins or flickers when even a trickle passes through.
  4. Wait 15 minutes without using waterSet a timer. A longer wait, such as an hour, catches slow leaks better.
  5. Read it againCompare the numbers and watch the indicator. Any change at all means water moved while nothing was meant to be using it.

If the numbers moved, repeat the test once to rule out a neighbor flushing or a missed appliance. A second moving reading is a real signal.

How to read what you see

The result points you toward the right next step. The table below sorts the most common outcomes, and the third column is what a plumber would try first.

What the meter doesLikely meaningSensible next step
Indicator spins steadilyActive leak, often sizableClose the main inside the home and call for help
Indicator crawls or ticks now and thenSlow leak or a toilet seeping into the bowlDo the toilet dye check, then book a trace
Reading moves only while a toilet is fillingRunning toilet flapperRepair the toilet before assuming a pipe leak
Nothing moves for an hourNo leak at that momentLook at fixtures that leak only under use

If the meter stops moving once you close the main shutoff inside your home, the leak is on the house side of that valve. If it keeps moving with that valve closed, the loss is between the meter and the valve, or the shutoff itself is not sealing. Either case is worth a professional look, and the second one often points to the supply line from the street.

Rule out the cheap culprits first

Toilets cause more phantom meter movement than buried pipes do. Add a few drops of food coloring to each tank and leave the bathroom alone for 15 minutes. If color appears in the bowl without a flush, the flapper is leaking, and a toilet repair usually fixes the problem in one visit.

Check under every sink and behind the washer for damp cabinet floors, swollen trim or a musty smell. Look at the water heater's relief valve discharge pipe for drips, and walk the yard looking for soft, unusually green or squishy ground between the house and the street. A water heater that weeps from its tank is a separate fix, covered by water heater repair.

What Newark residents should know before testing

Local note, Newark and New Castle County

Newark's public water utility maintains roughly 8,700 meters, according to the City's own water pages, and is rolling out smart meters that the City says read monthly. It names easier leak spotting as a benefit of the change. The same pages say City staff contact customers when they suspect a leak, and will try to help find the source if you ask.

So a moving meter gives you a useful call before you spend anything. Phone the City's water department and ask whether your account was flagged, and what it can tell you about the source. Then pass that to your plumber.

Two limits apply. The City has no published leak billing adjustment policy or water supply line boundary that we can point you to, so ask the City directly rather than assume. Separately, the City's code does say that for a frozen service line, the owner is responsible from the curb box shutoff valve onward into the property.

If you need the street-side valve closed while a repair is scheduled, the City can shut the water off at the curb once the repair is booked. Your own interior main shutoff is the faster option in a real emergency, so make sure you know where it is before you need it.

When a meter test points to the yard or the slab

A leak between the street and the house shows up as a moving meter with every fixture off and sometimes a wet patch, a sinking spot in the lawn or a dip in pressure. Slab leaks add warm floor patches or a faint hiss at night. Neither is a do-it-yourself repair, because the pipe is buried or under concrete and a wrong dig can hit gas, electric or other lines.

Delaware's one-call system, reached through call811.com, handles marking underground utilities before any digging. A plumber handling a buried supply line will often need that done first, and sometimes a permit as well. For a failing pipe between the street and your home, the next page to read is water service line repair.

What to do while you wait for a plumber

  • Close the main shutoff inside the house if the meter spins with everything off.
  • Move rugs, boxes and electronics off any wet floor or wall, and keep power cords out of standing water.
  • Take a photo of the meter reading and the time, because it helps whoever traces the leak.
  • Do not run the dishwasher or washer while you wait, since both add noise to the test.
  • Write down where you saw damp, stains or soft ground, even if you are not sure it matters.

Clogged Sewer Line Repair in Newark, DE: Who Is Responsible and What Happens Next

Newark's sewer code splits the work between the City and the owner, and the split depends on where the clog is and whether a cleanout exists.

Written by Newark 24/7 Plumbing · 2026

When sewage backs up in a Newark, DE home, call the City's Water and Wastewater office first and ask for a check of the main under the street. If the main is clear, the stoppage is on the lateral (the pipe running from the house to the main) or the house line, and you hire a licensed plumber to find which section.

Sec. 25-2 decides who pays for each stretch. That order saves you from paying to dig up something the City already owns.

While you wait, stop running water, keep everyone away from floor drains and move belongings off a wet basement floor. We dispatch 24/7, so an after-hours call is possible if the City confirms the main is clear.

The rest of this guide explains how the code divides the work, how a clogged line gets diagnosed, and when a clog turns into sewer line repair in Newark, DE rather than simple clearing.

Who owns which part of the pipe

Newark's sewer code, Sec. 25-2, separates owner and City duties by location and by cause. It is more detailed than the common shorthand that the homeowner owns everything from the house to the main, so the specifics are worth reading.

SituationWho handles it under Sec. 25-2Why it matters
Stoppage caused by grease, soap or other material from the houseOwnerThe lateral stoppage is yours to clear
Roots in the lateral when there is no cleanout near the property lineOwnerWithout that access point, the clearing is yours
Cracked or broken pipe from the property line to the houseOwnerRepair is on your side of the line
Roots from the property line out to the main, with a cleanout near the lineCityHaving the cleanout shifts that stretch to the City
Failed lateral from the property line out to the mainCityFixing or replacing that stretch is the City's job

If roots can only come out of the right-of-way stretch by digging, the code says the City handles the excavation, root removal and pipe repair in the right-of-way only. An owner can also ask the City to manage roots entering from the right-of-way by installing a cleanout on the property line.

Read that as a map, not a verdict. A plumber with a camera can show which section holds the problem, and the City confirms its side. Ask both before you accept a bill for the street section.

Signs the clog is in the sewer line

A fixture clog stays at one drain. A sewer line clog shows up across the house.

Several fixtures at once

A tub gurgles when the washing machine drains, or a toilet bubbles when you run the sink.

Backup in the lowest drain

Water or sewage appears in a basement floor drain or shower first because it is the lowest opening.

Slow flushing after the plunger

The toilet clears but refills slowly, then clogs again within a day.

Repeat clogs after cleaning

The line opens, then fails again in a few weeks, which often points to roots or a damaged section.

Sewage odor outside

A smell near the cleanout or a soggy patch in the yard can mean the line is broken.

What the City checks, and what the plumber does

The City's guide says it will send a crew to inspect the main under the street. If the main is clear, you are on your own to hire a plumber. Expect the plumber to start by finding the cleanout, then clearing with a cable machine or a water jet, then running a camera to see why the line clogged.

The camera step matters. A one-time grease clog needs cleaning, while a collapsed section, an offset joint or a root intrusion needs repair. A plumber who clears the line without looking leaves you guessing whether it will fail again, so ask for a sewer camera inspection when the line has clogged more than once.

Repair or clearing: how the options compare

Clearing removes the blockage. Repair fixes the pipe that let it form. The right option depends on what the camera shows, and cost depends on depth, length, access and whether the work crosses paved areas or trees, so there is no honest flat answer. We cannot name a price here, and the plumber who looks at your line will quote a clear price before work starts.

For a short damaged section, a spot repair or excavation may be enough. For longer runs, some plumbers offer trenchless methods that avoid trenching across a yard, though they suit only certain pipe conditions. If the line has bellied or sagged so it holds water, no lining will fix it, because the dip stays.

What Newark property owners should know

Local note, Newark and New Castle County

The City of Newark cares for over 100 miles of sanitary sewer, and its Water Resources staff say the department is available 24/7 for backups. The City also warns that it usually cannot pay for cleanup or damage after a backup. Policies often omit sewage backup unless you add a rider. Ask your insurance agent whether you carry it. The Delaware Department of Insurance lists sewer backup coverage as optional extra protection.

Two code points help you plan. When a lateral is replaced or a new sewer is installed, Newark requires a cleanout at the property line, and swapping out hidden waste pipe for new material is treated as new work, so it needs a permit and an inspection. Permits inside the city come from Code Enforcement for parcels whose number begins with 18, and the County handles the rest.

The City also recommends a backflow device or blocking a floor drain to reduce backup risk. If an owner fails to fix a violation after a ten-day notice, the code lets the City do the work and bill the owner, with the cost added to the tax bill if unpaid.

That permit point is easy to miss. Before a plumber digs up your yard, ask who is pulling the permit. Our Bear and Elkton pages cover areas outside the City, where permits and sewer rules come from different offices.

New Castle County also runs a home repair program that lists sewer lines among eligible repairs for qualified homeowners. The county page does not say whether homes inside Newark city limits qualify, so ask the County directly before you count on it.

Keeping the line clear afterward

Most repeat clogs come from habits and trees. Keep grease, rags, wipes, diapers and sanitary products out of the line. If roots keep returning, a property-line cleanout lets the City manage roots in its stretch and gives a plumber a clean access point. Regular sewer line cleaning helps when roots are the cause, though no official source sets a fixed interval.

Downtown Newark is the commercial and mixed-use center of the city. According to the City's development plan, the downtown along Main Street and Delaware Avenue is the commercial heart of Newark, with a downtown business zone running between East Main Street and Delaware Avenue.

This page is for businesses and for the people who live above and around them, and the Newark service area page shows the rest of our coverage. It is a commercial district page, not a residential subdivision page.

Why a downtown building plumbs differently

The city was chartered in 1758, and the blocks along Main Street hold a mix of building ages. We do not know the age or pipe material of any particular building, and we will not guess. What we can say is that mixed-use buildings often have shop space at street level, apartments above and one set of drains below.

In that setup, a clog at the lowest point shows up in the ground-floor business first, even when the cause is a fixture upstairs. A camera run along the drain tells you which line is at fault before anyone cuts open a floor. Our sewer camera inspection page describes what the camera shows, and our commercial plumbing page covers building-wide work.

Kitchens, grease and slow drains

For a restaurant, the drain you use hardest is the one most likely to slow down. The City's Sewer Care Guide warns against sending fats, oils and grease into drains. Grease cools and sticks to the pipe wall, so a line can narrow for months before it stops. A kitchen drain that quits mid-service is a business emergency, and our 24/7 line takes that call at any hour.

A cable can open a clogged line, but a water jet scours the pipe wall as well. Our hydro jetting page explains when the extra step is worth it. If a kitchen has a grease trap, it needs regular cleaning by whoever services it, and a plumber can check that the lines downstream are clear.

Landlords, tenants and student rentals

The City's housing plan describes the downtown zone as allowing high-density rental housing, and the University of Delaware sits beside it. A Main Street building can therefore serve several parties at once: an owner, a business tenant and residents above. That is why a leak call often needs a landlord's approval and a key.

Upstairs apartments bring Delaware's rental licensing rule into play, and the state's license lookup shows who is allowed to do the work. Keep a written record of what the plumber found and what was done, because an insurer or the City may ask for it. Section-by-section responsibility depends on the lease, so check it before the call.

What to do right now

  • Close the building or suite shutoff if water is leaking, and call the 24/7 line.
  • Move stock, files and electronics away from the water.
  • Take photos of the damage for the insurer and the landlord.
  • Stop using floor drains and sinks that are backing up.
  • Line up a key or a contact so the plumber can reach mechanical rooms and cleanouts.
  • Gas odor? Clear the building, then contact 911 and the gas utility from outside before anyone phones a plumber.

Permits and water service

Permits and inspections for work inside the city run through the City's Code Enforcement office on South Main Street. Parcel numbers decide who issues the permit: the City's page says one starting with 18 goes through the City, so confirm yours first.

Then ask them what a water heater swap or a new fixture needs, because commercial work can involve more than a home job does. The City's Building Permits page lists the 2018 International Plumbing Code among its adopted codes, so ask which edition governs your job.

The City supplies water and runs the sewer mains here. If the sewer main under the street is the problem, the City clears it. When the main is clear, the lateral is the building owner's job. Our page on how a heater swap works covers that job.

Oaklands is a residential neighborhood inside the City of Newark, near Hillside Park and central Newark. The City's own planning documents name it, which is why this page treats it as a real part of town and not a marketing label. We describe it by landmarks instead of boundary lines, because the City does not publish a street-by-street border for it. If your street is near the edge, our Newark service area page shows how far our dispatch reaches.

What the City's plan says about the age of the area

In its adopted plan, the City dates many of the neighborhoods draining to the Hillside Park pond to the 1950s and 1960s. It adds that the pond collects runoff from Oaklands and neighboring Nottingham Green, roughly 70 acres in all, and that stormwater in these areas was designed to move water quickly toward the creeks.

For you, the age of the area is a reason to check, not a reason to assume. The plan does not say what pipe is in any house, and neither do we. A camera run through the main drain shows the real material and condition, and our sewer camera inspection page explains what the plumber looks for and what you receive afterward.

Home listings for the area describe mostly detached houses, with many dating to around the early 1960s. Treat that as a general picture. A house that has been renovated may have newer drains and supply lines than its neighbors.

Hillside Park and the water around it

Hillside Park is a 7.24-acre park at 151 Forest Lane, on the site of the former Rodney Dormitory. The City finished it in 2021, and it includes a stormwater management facility along with walking paths and a fishing pier. The City's parks chapter says the project improved access to the park from the Oaklands neighborhood.

That pond matters to a homeowner for one practical reason. Rain that leaves your yard and street ends up in a managed system, but water that enters your own basement does not. Heavy rain can still push water through foundation cracks, so a house in a drainage area is worth checking for a working sump pump and a clear floor drain. Our sump pump service page covers testing and replacement.

Who fixes a sewer backup: the City or you

Newark's Sewer Care Guide splits the responsibility clearly. The City maintains the sanitary sewer main under the street, and the homeowner owns the lateral, which is the pipe linking the house to that main. A blockage in the lateral is yours to get cleared, according to the guide.

If you are not sure which side the problem is on, call the City's Water Resources line first, which the guide describes as available around the clock, and call us as well. A plumber can find out whether the water is standing in your cleanout. If it is, the blockage is likely downstream of your house. Our sewer line cleaning page explains how a cable or a water jet clears the lateral.

The same guide notes that the City generally cannot cover backup damage, and suggests fitting a backflow device that keeps sewage from returning into a house. Many homeowner policies also exclude sewage backup, so read yours before you need it.

First steps while you wait

  • Close the main water valve if a pipe or tank is leaking.
  • Stop using sinks, tubs and the washing machine if drains are backing up.
  • Switch off power only if the panel is dry and you can reach it safely.
  • Lift belongings off the floor and take photos of what the water reached.
  • Smell gas? Get everyone outside first, then phone 911 and the gas utility from the street, and only then call us.

Permits, water and the paperwork

Permits in Newark follow the parcel number. A number beginning with 18 means the City of Newark issues the permit, and any other number sends you to New Castle County. For work such as a water heater swap, ask the City's Code Enforcement staff on South Main Street which rules apply to your address. See our water heater replacement page for what the job involves.

The City runs the public water system here, so your bill names your supplier. We do not quote water hardness for the area because we could not read a published figure. A cheap test kit at your own tap gives you a real number, and it tells you whether scale could be shortening the life of your heater.

If your house was built before 1978, the EPA's lead-safe rule applies to certain renovation work, and the City's permit page says contractors must be EPA-certified for it. Ask any plumber about it before they open a wall. You can check a plumber's license on the state's license lookup.

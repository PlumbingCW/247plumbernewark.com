A leak in an attached home does not stay in one house. If water is coming through a shared wall or ceiling, close your main valve, keep clear of wet outlets and call. In College Park, Newark, DE, our emergency line is staffed 24/7 and a licensed plumber comes to your door.

College Park is a residential area in West Newark, and the City's housing plan lists it among its townhome areas with row-home zoning, alongside White Chapel and Blair Court. The plan describes these as higher-density parts of the city. We place College Park by that planning description and not by a hard boundary, because the City does not publish one. Detached houses nearby in Oaklands raise different questions, and the whole city falls inside our Newark service area.

Why attached homes plumb differently

In a detached house, a burst supply line mostly damages that house. In a townhome or a row home, water follows the wall cavity, the floor joists or the pipe chase, and it can reach the unit next door before you notice. That is general plumbing knowledge, not a claim about a particular building in College Park.

So the first minutes matter more. Learn where your own shutoff is, because it may be in a utility closet, a crawl space or behind a panel. Tell your neighbor and your landlord or association right away, and take photos before anything is moved. A leak detection visit can then trace where the water is coming from, using methods that do not require tearing out whole walls.

What the housing mix means for repairs

Home listings and neighborhood guides describe College Park as townhouses, duplexes and small apartment buildings, mostly built between about 1960 and 1980. Take that as a general picture. Individual buildings have been renovated, and the age of a unit tells you little about its pipes without looking.

According to the City's housing plan, about half of Newark's homes are rented, with 52 percent owner-occupied in 2019 across the whole city. That is a citywide number, and it is not specific to College Park. It does explain why repair calls here often involve a tenant, an owner and sometimes an association, each with a different job.

Water heaters in tight closets

Townhomes often have the water heater in a small closet or utility room. A leaking tank in that space can soak drywall and flooring in a short time, so a puddle at the base is an emergency. The fix is usually replacement, and we explain the decision on our water heater repair page.

Code editions differ by source: the City's Building Permits page lists the 2018 International Plumbing Code, and the state regulation adopts a newer edition. Space also limits what can be installed. A heat pump heater needs open air around it, which many closets do not offer. Ask the plumber to measure and check venting before any unit is ordered, and ask the City's permit office on South Main Street which permit applies to your address.

Drains and who owns what

Whether the lateral serving your row is shared is something we cannot tell you from the street. Your deed, your association documents or the City's records can answer it. The City does say that it looks after the sewer main under the street, while the lateral from the house to that main belongs to the homeowner.

When several units drain into the same line, a blockage shows up in the lowest unit first. Slow drains in a ground-floor bath and a basement while upstairs sinks run fine is a typical sign. A cable or water jet can clear it, and our drain cleaning page explains how the plumber chooses between them.

While you wait for the plumber

  1. Shut the water offTurn your main valve to closed, or close the shutoff under the fixture that is leaking.
  2. Protect the electricsStay away from wet outlets and switch off a breaker only if the panel is dry.
  3. Warn the neighborsKnock on the adjoining unit and tell your landlord or association.
  4. Document the damageTake photos and note the time the leak began for any insurance claim.
  5. Smell gas?Walk everyone out, ring 911 and the gas company from the sidewalk, and leave the plumber call until after that.

Licensing and rental property

Rented units fall under Delaware's plumbing license rules, so hire only someone who appears on the state's license lookup. Tenants should call the landlord as well as the plumber. If you own the unit, keep the repair invoice, because an association or insurer may ask for it.
